Quick Line Fault Detection and Identiﬁcation Using
High-Fidelity Models of Transient Dynamics
Austin Warner, Student Member, IEEE, Dakota Hamilton, Student Member, IEEE,
Georgios Fellouris, Member, IEEE, and Dionysios Aliprantis, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—A robust, versatile algorithm is proposed for quick
detection and identiﬁcation of transmission line faults. Our
approach is based on time-domain simulations of power system
transient dynamics using high-ﬁdelity models. The proposed al-
gorithm operates on real-time data from available PMU sensors,
which can be sparsely placed and located far from the line fault.
The algorithm successfully identiﬁes faults during the transient
regime, while avoiding false alarms. It is also shown to be
robust against uncertainties in operational parameters, as well
as adverse events such as sudden load changes. A case study is
included to illustrate algorithm design, tuning, and performance.
Index Terms—Change detection algorithms, fault detection,
phasor measurement units, power system dynamics, power sys-
tem faults, power system simulation.
I. INTRODUCTION
S
ITUATIONAL awareness is essential for the reliable and
secure operation of power systems. This term includes
an array of technologies that provide precise information
regarding the current system state and its anticipated evolution
over time, including actions taken to restore or maintain
normal operation [1]. Due to the highly interconnected nature
of modern power networks, transmission system operators
(TSOs) must also be aware of the state of neighboring systems
that may affect the reliability of their control area. Poor situa-
tional awareness and lack of information sharing across TSO
boundaries have been signiﬁcant contributing factors in several
recent blackouts across the United States and Europe [2]–[4].
Recent advancements in power system measurement and
monitoring technology, such as the proliferation of phasor
measurement units (PMUs), provide new opportunities for
improving situational awareness in power systems. PMUs pro-
vide Global Positioning System (GPS) time-stamped voltage
and current phasor data (magnitude and phase angle) at various
locations around the network.
The use of PMU data to quickly detect and identify line
faults in transmission networks is of growing interest in the
power systems literature [5]–[12]. In [5], the changes in phase
angles are monitored and compared against their expected be-
havior following a transmission line fault, which is computed

The goal is to detect this change as quickly as possible
while maintaining a desired false alarm rate [13]. Applied to
situational awareness in power systems, QCD methodology is
used to detect transmission line faults by monitoring PMU
data streams for changes characterized by a line fault.
The algorithms developed in [5], [6] are based on steady-
state or quasi-steady-state power ﬂow models that are valid
on the order of minutes to hours. That is, it is assumed that
the fast dynamics of the system have settled out before a
line outage can be detected. However, power system transient
dynamics (developing over sub-second timescales) are often
critical in ensuring system stability. Therefore, it is important
to quickly detect and identify faults within these timescales,
allowing system operators to anticipate the evolution of system
dynamics and take appropriate actions. In [10], a method is
proposed for line outage detection at fast timescales using
the bus impedance matrix, but the transient dynamics of the
system are not explicitly modeled.
Some recent works have attempted to consider the transient
response of the system in line outage detection algorithms,
leveraging the high-frequency (30–120 Hz), time-synchronized
sampling of PMU sensors. In [7], the modeling in [6] is ex-
tended to include time-dependent participation factor matrices
that model the impact of governor dynamics on changes in
generator power output, and a QCD procedure is proposed
to incorporate the transient dynamics of the fault. A small-
signal, time-variant model of the system dynamics is obtained
by taking the derivative of the nonlinear, ac power ﬂow
equations with respect to time in [8]. In [11], the generator
swing equations are introduced to incorporate the rotor me-
chanical dynamics in the proposed sparse recovery algorithm.
Other works have addressed the scalability of the QCD-based
approach [9], as well as the detection and identiﬁcation of
multiple simultaneous line outages [11], [12].
