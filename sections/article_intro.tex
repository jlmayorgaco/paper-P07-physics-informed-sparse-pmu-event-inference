\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\PARstart{A}{} load at bus~16 returns to its original setting.
While the generators are still swinging, a phasor measurement unit (PMU)
on a neighboring branch records another change in current. The change may
follow from the load recovery alone. It may also reflect the opening of
line 16--17 during that recovery. Can a monitor with eight PMUs distinguish
these cases and, if the line opened, identify it before it recloses?

A line opening changes the network admittance immediately. Reclosure restores
that admittance, but the rotor angles and speeds still reflect the outage.
We therefore treat recovery as a competing explanation for each new report.
A bank of switched differential-algebraic equation (DAE) models predicts
the next PMU report for continued recovery and for each candidate load
change or line switch during recovery. Each candidate is
weighted by how well its predicted reports match the observations. For
each source, the active-history weight supports a new-event alarm, while
the weight summed over active and restored histories supports the source
diagnosis. Thus a restored load can explain a transient without being
reported as still active
(Fig.~\ref{fig:problem-method}).

We test this distinction on successive, nonoverlapping interventions from
a fixed catalog of nine load buses and nine lines. We first compare the bank
with a continuous generalized likelihood ratio (GLR) observer and
previously fitted ExtraTrees adapters, using a common calibration target
for alarms in normal operation. A separate experiment refits learned
baselines on the bank's simulation cohort. We then ask what information
a monitor needs to keep after it
reports restoration. On paired sequences from two simulated plants, we
compare retaining recovery histories, merging them into a single state
mean, and resetting the physical part of that mean. We count an intervention
as correctly attributed when the monitor names its family and asset before
the intervention ends. We also count alarms that cannot be matched to an
intervention.
