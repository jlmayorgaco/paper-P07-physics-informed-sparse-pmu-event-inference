\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\PARstart{A}{fter} a load is restored or a line is reclosed, physical state
continues to evolve. A second intervention may begin before that response
decays. With eight PMUs, the observed change can combine recovery from the
earlier event with forcing from a new source
(Fig.~\ref{fig:problem-method}(a)).

The attribution problem is to determine whether the incoming reports
require a new source and, if so, which asset changed. Classical sequential
change detection provides tools for identifying changes in observation laws
\cite{nikiforov1995generalized,lai2000sequential,tartakovsky2014sequential}.
Willsky and Jones use the time evolution of innovations to detect jumps
in a linear system \cite{willsky1976glr}. Power-system outage
detectors compare network hypotheses under quasi-static or linearized
models \cite{tate2008outage,chen2016quickest,rovatsos2017outage}, and
trajectory libraries diagnose faults from sparse PMUs
\cite{warner2023highfidelity}. Here the observation law depends on the
earlier intervention because its electrical response persists after restoration.

We develop a bank of switched-DAE predictors that carries the estimated
state through restoration. Each history specifies a source, onset and
restoration time; load severity is integrated analytically. Separate active
and cause weights allow an intervention to end while its response remains
an explanation of later reports (Section~\ref{sec:narrative-method}).

Two comparisons connect this formulation to the evidence. The first
uses nominal signatures and a fixed-threshold reset control to examine
source separation and the innovation bias caused by discarding the
physical state (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The second measures timely
attribution and extra alarms against a continuous physical GLR and
ExtraTrees on held-out sequences from the known load and line catalog
(Section~\ref{sec:recovery-comparison}). Each procedure is calibrated to
a target of one normal alarm per hour. The comparison evaluates complete
procedures, so it leaves open how much of the difference comes from
representing recovery as separate histories.
