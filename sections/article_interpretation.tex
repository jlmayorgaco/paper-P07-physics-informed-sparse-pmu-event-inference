\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Physics Explains}
The information analysis turns the headline accuracy from an empirical
observation into a consequence of the network equations. Under the noise of
the reference data, every catalog intervention carries hundreds to tens of
millions of times the evidence needed for an alarm within one second, so
detection is limited by the publication rule rather than by information.
Line alarms arrive at the confirmation floor, as predicted. Load alarms
arrive about \RevLoadExcessReports{} reports later than the information
allows. This latency is not explained by the information and is therefore
algorithmic; the likely sources are the onset-proposal screen and the
competition among severity intervals, which could be changed without new
measurements. The errors that remain occur under out-of-bank R/X, where the
predicted signature itself is wrong. The same analysis predicts where
attribution should stop working, at the bus-31 load once the noise grows by
a factor of about \RevBusThirtyOneCross, and why forgetting the physical
state is costly everywhere: the free recovery response projected on the
PMUs is two to seven orders of magnitude larger than the alarm evidence.
Physical-state continuity is therefore not a tuning choice but a
requirement implied by the size of that response.

\subsection{Relation to Learned and Statistical Detectors}
The learned adapters and the physical bank differ in how they obtain source
responses: from labeled trajectories, or from the DAE operators. The
adapters reproduce the line responses but miss most load changes, a
pattern already present in their development validation. The comparison
therefore shows that a physically derived dictionary attributes small load
changes that these adapters do not, at the cost of more alarms when the
network model is wrong. It does not establish superiority over a learned
detector calibrated to a common alarm budget. Similarly, the adapter of
Chen \emph{et al.} fails here because its quasi-static white-noise model
does not match the plant, not because quickest-change detection is
unsuitable; an information-matched version of that test would use the
switched-DAE signatures of Section~\ref{sec:theory}.

\subsection{Limits of the Evidence}
\emph{Shared model family.} Observations and predictors come from the same
research plant. Nonlinear switching, noise and out-of-bank R/X challenge
the linearized inverse model, but transfer to a different dynamic model or
to field data is untested. The nine load and nine line candidates were
fixed during method development; they form a declared catalog, not a
sample of all assets or event families.

\emph{Measurement realism.} The noise model reproduces the reference data,
whose differential angle noise is small. Independent per-PMU noise of
0.05--0.1\% TVE, well inside the standard's limits, suppresses most load
attributions of the implemented bank even though the angles still carry
ample information (Section~\ref{sec:noise-stress}). Field use therefore
requires estimating the cross-PMU noise covariance, not only its scale,
from the startup prefix; this extension was not tested here. The terminal map
retains a provisional BUS5 current terminal and excludes the BUS39 current
angle; it is a research measurement hypothesis, not a verified
installation. Cross-family noise covariance is not applied.

\emph{Approximation.} Pruning, steady-state covariances, plug-in startup
estimates and mean compression at closure make the bank approximate; its
masses are attribution weights, not calibrated probabilities. Retrospective
proposals update the history posterior but not the network-model weights.
The birth and restoration hazards are conservative relative to the test
schedules; their effect is bounded by \eqref{eq:alarm-evidence} but was
not swept.

\emph{Operation.} The startup prefix must be event-free, all PMUs available
and the noise within the implemented support. The nominal bank runs faster
than the input on one thread; the five-model bank does not. These results
support diagnostic development, not protection or control deployment.
