\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\PARstart{P}{hasor} measurement units (PMUs) reveal electromechanical transients through synchronized voltage and current phasors, yet transmission networks instrument only a fraction of their buses and branches \cite{phadke2008synchronized,zhao2019dse}. A disturbance may begin outside the measured region, alter hidden voltages, and propagate to the available devices. Missing frames and corrupted channels create a second ambiguity because they can resemble physical transients. Diagnosis must therefore separate three questions: what is the network state, which event occurred, and where did each applicable physical or measurement event originate?

Dynamic estimators combine network models with synchrophasors \cite{zhao2019dse,katanic2024recursive}. Sparse-PMU classifiers identify events and faulted assets \cite{pandey2020realtime,yildiz2024few}. Other learning methods reconstruct hidden trajectories before localization or estimate state and attack location from partial observations \cite{rimkus2025gnn,zhai2025partial}. These methods solve different inverse problems. The unresolved case considered here has an under-observed state, concurrent physical and measurement-integrity changes, source assets absent from labeled training, and sensing geometries that may render two sources indistinguishable.

The distinction is operational, not semantic. A generation change can be visible throughout the network while its source remains ambiguous. A dropped PMU identifies an integrity location directly but removes information needed to localize a simultaneous physical event. Likewise, a state estimate can have small point error while the intervention that produced it is not uniquely identifiable. A single multiclass label conceals these differences and produces accuracy values whose denominators do not match the claimed task.

A physical candidate is represented by $h=(c,\ell,\tau,\gamma)$, containing the event family, source, onset, and magnitude. Measurement availability and corruption remain separate variables. Each candidate generates a counterfactual DAE response, while candidate comparison fits admissible initial-state and integrity nuisance directions. The distance that remains after this fit measures source separation. A zero distance identifies a pair for which a uniformly correct source decision is impossible under the declared local model.

Two independent studies supply complementary evidence. The PowerDynamics study reconstructs 31 hidden complex bus voltages from eight voltage/current PMUs and tests operating-point mismatch. The ANDES study evaluates a physics-guided hierarchical ExtraTrees baseline on eight abnormal labels plus normal operation. They use the same PMU buses and related IEEE 39-bus networks, but their simulator semantics, state coordinates, and data splits differ. Their metrics are kept separate.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a hybrid DAE inference model that maps eight event labels to composable physical-intervention and measurement-integrity variables;
  \item a finite-horizon analysis that separates target-state estimability from event-source diagnosability, including a minimum-variance covariance, a sparse-integrity margin, and a nonlinear remainder certificate; and
  \item a paired evaluation showing accurate hidden-voltage reconstruction and operating-point recentering, together with a decisive failure of learned localization under complete physical-source holdout.
\end{enumerate}

The empirical boundary is sharp. The state estimator reaches \BTwoTVE{} mean hidden-voltage HVRE, while physical recentering closes \EZeroHClosureHalf{}--\EZeroHClosureMax{} of the operating-point error gap. The known-source event model detects and classifies disturbances, but complete source holdout leaves only \HeldSourceCorrect{} correct decisions among \HeldSourceDecisions{}. Thus state recovery and event recognition do not imply source recovery. Candidate-conditioned physical inference is the next experiment, not an accomplished result.
